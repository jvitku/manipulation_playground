The question

Tasks 6 and 7 used self-attention: every token asks a question of the other tokens of the same sequence. Many problems have two sequences instead. A translator reads a sentence and writes another one; a robot policy reads a history of observations and writes a chunk of actions. The writer needs to look at the reader's notes, but the notes are not part of its own sequence. Cross-attention is attention where the questions come from one sequence and the keys and values from another. This task is the smallest honest case: rewrite a date written three different ways as an ISO date, one character at a time, and watch which source character the decoder reads for each character it writes.

The task

The source is a date between 1970 and 2070 in one of three formats, drawn uniformly: 23 Oct 2026, Oct 23, 2026 or 23/10/2026. The target is always 2026-10-23. The model works on characters (36 symbols plus padding, start and end markers: a vocabulary of 39). The 36 890 days are split by day with a fixed hash: 33 200 days for training and 3 690 held out, so a day is never in both. The chart held-out exact-match decodes held-out days with the model's own output and counts a date as right only if all ten characters match, so it cannot be passed by memorising the training dates. Chance is essentially 0: the first measured value of the default run is 0.0.

The loss is the cross-entropy of every target character. Training uses teacher forcing: the decoder is fed <bos> followed by the true target characters, shifted right by one, and at every position must predict the next one (and finally <eos>), all positions in one parallel pass:

\(x\) is the source, \(y_t\) the target character at position \(t\), padding positions are ignored. At test time there is no target: greedy_decode starts from <bos> alone and feeds back its own most likely character until it emits <eos>.

The method: where Q, K and V come from

In self-attention one sequence produces all three. In cross-attention they come from two places. With \(X\) the decoder's hidden states (one row per output position) and \(M\) the encoder's output, the memory (one row per source character):

$$Q = X W_Q,\qquad K = M W_K,\qquad V = M W_V,\qquad A = \operatorname{softmax}\!\left(\frac{Q K^{\top}}{\sqrt{d_k}}\right),\qquad \text{out} = A\,V$$

\(A\) has one row per output position and one column per source position (11 × 12 here): row \(t\) says how much of each source character output \(t\) reads. That matrix is the alignment the app draws. Nothing else changes from task 6: the same MHA code in lab/tasks/minitf.py is called with x_q = X and x_kv = M instead of one input twice.

The architecture

The wiring is the whole idea: the arrow into the cross-attention box labelled Q comes from the decoder's own stream; the arrow labelled K, V comes from the encoder's memory. The loss sits only at the decoder's output; the encoder is trained solely through the K and V it supplies. The models are 1 encoder Block and 1 CrossBlock (masked self-attention, cross-attention, MLP), width d_model and heads as set; the defaults (64, 4) have 125 863 parameters. The shapes shown are for the defaults of the date formats: 12 source positions (padded), 11 decoder positions (<bos> plus the 10 target characters; the 11th output is <eos>).

What to watch

While training, the live attention panel under the charts shows one heatmap per head: the cross-attention of a fixed probe pair (Oct 23, 2026 → 2026-10-23, teacher-forced), rows = decoder positions, columns = source characters (hover for the character). It starts as a near-uniform grey and organises as the loss falls. After training, evaluate a checkpoint: the viewer shows the alignment matrix for held-out dates with the character on both axes (pick the head), plus the decoder's and encoder's self-attention, and a decode step slider that outlines the row used to emit each output character — slide it and read which source character was fetched at each step.

Measured in this app (defaults: d_model 64, 4 heads, 1500 steps, batch 128, lr 2e-3, GPU shared with other jobs, about 12 s a run; seeds 0, 1, 2):

  • Exact-match on unseen days is 100% (2000 held-out day/format pairs, all three seeds). The curve is a plateau followed by a cliff: loss 3.84 at step 5 (a uniform guess over 39 characters is \(\ln 39 = 3.66\)), 2.88 at step 25, 1.46 at step 50, 0.92 at step 75 (exact-match still 0.0), 0.10 at step 125, 0.022 at step 150 where the held-out exact-match is already 0.998, then 0.0005 at step 1500. In 500-step runs the first check at or above 95% came at step 165, 135 and 135 for the three seeds.
  • The model is no bigger than it needs to be: 1 head at d_model 32 and 2 heads at d_model 64 also score 100% (2000 examples, seed 0).
  • The decoder really aligns. Measured on 500 held-out days per format, teacher-forced: the share of a head's cross-attention weight that falls on the source characters of the field being written (year, month or day) is, averaged over the four heads, 0.60–0.75 for the year (4 of the 12 source positions, so uniform attention would give 0.33), 0.48–0.71 for the month (uniform 0.17–0.25) and 0.58–0.83 for the day (uniform 0.17). The best single head reaches 0.69–0.99 on every field and format. The mean of each row's largest weight is 0.67–0.78 against 0.083 for uniform attention. Heads divide the work rather than all doing the same: on the live probe (Oct 23, 2026, seed 0) the units digit of the day, "3" at source column 5, is the peak of all four heads (weights 0.79–1.00), the last two year digits (columns 10 and 11) are the peak of heads 0 and 2, and the other rows are spread across heads and neighbouring columns. A head may also park on a padding column when it has nothing to fetch: in the first evaluation example (11/10/2046), head 0 puts 87% of its weight on a padding column while the month's second digit is written.
  • The format never matters to the result: the same output position points at a different source column per format ("26" of the year is column 10–11 in Oct 23, 2026 but 8–9 in 23/10/2026), and the alignment moves with it. That is what cross-attention buys over a fixed mapping: the content of the question, not the position, selects the answer.

Where cross-attention is used

  • Sequence-to-sequence models. The original encoder–decoder Transformer for translation has this exact layout in every decoder layer, with the encoder run once and its memory re-read at every output step; speech models that write text from an audio encoder do the same.
  • Perceiver-style latent queries. A small, fixed array of learned vectors (the queries) cross-attends into a very large input (the keys and values), so the cost grows with the input only once, linearly, and the latents become a fixed-size summary of it. Q comes from the learned array, K and V from the data.
  • Vision-language models. Some of them let text tokens cross-attend into image features; others instead place the image tokens in the same sequence as the text and use self-attention. They are two ways to the same read.
  • ACT in this repository (Stage 1). ACTLite in src/fvb/policy/models.py is the policy that scripts/10_train_bc.py trains for the gantry insertion task (the force-token comparison of task 5 uses it). Its history of observation tokens goes through a nn.TransformerEncoder into a memory mem; K learned query vectors (self.queries) are run through a nn.TransformerDecoder as self.decoder(q, mem), i.e. the action-chunk queries cross-attend into the observation memory, and a linear head turns the result into K actions. Compared with this task: the queries are constants (the “slot” for action 0, 1, …), so there is no text being written one character at a time, no causal mask and no teacher forcing — all K actions come out in one pass — but Q from the decoder side and K, V from the encoder side is exactly the wiring above.

Relation to task 10 (ta-vla)

TAVLAMini (task 10) has no separate cross-attention module. It concatenates the prefix (image patches, words, state) and the suffix (the torque token and the noisy action chunk) into one sequence and uses a block attention mask: suffix queries see the prefix keys and values and the suffix's own; prefix queries see only the prefix. The suffix's read of the prefix is the same “Q from one stream, K and V from another” as here, only done inside one softmax (the suffix attends to prefix and suffix together) and expressed as a mask pattern instead of as a second attention layer. The practical payoff the lesson of task 10 describes, caching the prefix keys and values across denoising steps, is the same as running a cross-attention encoder once and re-reading its memory: it works because the memory side never looks back at the writer.

Knobs

  • Model width d_model and heads: width of the embeddings and number of parallel attention patterns (d_model must divide by heads). The cross-attention matrix has one map per head; they specialise (see above), so look at several.
  • Training steps, learning rate, seed: batch 128 and the split of days are fixed. Curves are flat for the first ~75 steps and then fall quickly, so very short runs show the plateau only.
  • Evaluation: the eval seed picks which held-out days are shown and the number of examples; the exact-match figure is always measured on 2000 held-out day/format pairs.

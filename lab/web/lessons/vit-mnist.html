The question

Task 6 showed attention as a lookup between a handful of hand-made tokens. Real inputs do not come as tokens: an image is a grid of pixels. The trick that turned transformers into image models is to make tokens out of it — cut the picture into small squares (patches), treat every patch as a word, and let attention work out which patches matter for the answer. The robot policy in task 10 (ta-vla) uses the same recipe on camera and force tokens. Here it is on the smallest vision problem there is: reading handwritten digits (MNIST), with a transformer small enough to train in seconds.

The task

The input is a 28×28 grey digit, the output one of 10 classes. Training uses train_n images (default 10 000 of the 60 000); the validation curve is measured on a fixed set of 1000 held-out test digits, so it is never trained on. Chance is 10% accuracy, loss \(\ln 10 \approx 2.30\). The loss is the cross-entropy of the class prediction:

$$\mathcal{L} = -\log p_\theta(\text{label}\mid\text{image})$$

The method

The image is cut into \(N = (28/\text{patch})^2\) patches (16 of 7×7 pixels by default, or 4 of 14×14), each flattened to a vector and embedded by one linear layer. Attention itself ignores order, so a learned position vector is added to every patch token; in front goes a learned CLS token that belongs to no patch:

$$x_0 = [\,c;\; p_1 W_E;\; \dots;\; p_N W_E\,] + E_\text{pos}\in\mathbb{R}^{(N+1)\times d_\text{model}}$$

Then depth transformer blocks (minitf.Block, shared with the next tasks) refine the tokens. Each block does self-attention — every token queries all tokens, as in task 6 — and then a small MLP, both wrapped in a LayerNorm and a residual connection:

$$x \leftarrow x + \operatorname{MHA}(\operatorname{LN}(x)),\qquad x \leftarrow x + \operatorname{MLP}(\operatorname{LN}(x))$$

The residual "+ x" lets a layer add information without erasing what is already there, and keeps gradients healthy through a deep stack. The final CLS vector, after one more LayerNorm and a linear layer, is the class score: only CLS is read out, so it has to collect from the patches whatever tells the digits apart. Where it collects from is its attention row, which is what the app draws.

The architecture

Shapes are for the defaults (patch 7: 16 patches + CLS = 17 tokens). With patch 14 there are 4 patches + CLS = 5 tokens of 196 pixels each. Per block the attention weights have shape (heads, tokens, tokens); the app draws only row 0, the CLS token's row.

What to watch

While the run trains, the live attention panel under the charts shows, for one fixed probe digit (the first test image, a 7), one picture per block and head: the digit with the patch grid over it, tinted orange where the CLS token attends (about 60 snapshots per run, each picture scaled to its own maximum). After training, evaluate a checkpoint: the viewer lets you step through random test digits and choose block and head, and reports the prediction, its confidence and how the head's weight splits between the CLS token itself and the patches.

Measured in this app (defaults: patch 7, 2 blocks, 2 heads, d_model 64, 10 000 images, 2000 steps, batch 128, lr 1e-3, GPU, 105 098 parameters, about 8 s):

  • Validation accuracy on the 1000 held-out digits: 71.2% after 132 steps, 83.0% at 264, 91.5% at 660 and then it flattens at 93.3% (final; 93.4% on all 10 000 test digits). Seed 1 ends at 92.2%.
  • The training loss goes from 2.51 (step 6) to 0.006, i.e. the model memorises its 10 000 training digits long before the validation curve stops; the gap between those two curves is the price of a small training set. More images, not more steps, closes it: this is what the training images knob is for.
  • Bigger patches did better here: patch 14 (just 4 patches + CLS) reached 95.6%, because a 14×14 patch already contains a whole stroke segment and the model has far fewer tokens to organise. A 1-block model with 2 heads reached 89.8% (below the 2-block 93.3%); 4 heads in 2 blocks 92.9%, no gain over 2 heads.
  • Where the CLS token looks (default run, 2000 test digits): the central 2×2 patches hold 65% of block 0 head 0's attention (25% if it were uniform) and 44%, 31%, 40% for block 0 head 1 and block 1 heads 0 and 1. Of the 16 patches 38% contain ink on average; the share of attention that goes to those inked patches is 71%, 65%, 49% and 58% for the same four heads. The CLS token gives only 3–7% of its weight to itself. So early attention is broadly "look at the middle where the stroke is"; the sharpest single patch of a head takes 22–47% of its weight. These are averages of what a head does, not a proof of what the classifier relies on.

Knobs

  • Patch size: 7 gives 16 tokens, 14 gives 4. Fewer, larger patches are cheaper and here more accurate; smaller ones give attention more to choose between and a sharper picture of where it looks.
  • Transformer blocks and heads: depth stacks attention-and-MLP rounds; heads split the width into parallel lookups (d_model must divide by heads, it does for 32, 64, 96, 128 with 2 or 4 heads).
  • Model width d_model, learning rate, steps, seed: the usual capacity and optimisation dials.
  • Training images: the subset of MNIST's 60 000 images used for training. Try 1000 against 60 000 and watch the validation curve; this model has no augmentation, so data is what generalisation comes from.

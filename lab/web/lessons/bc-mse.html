The question

What goes wrong when you clone an expert with plain regression? Behaviour cloning looks like the safe default: collect demonstrations, fit the actions with squared error, deploy. This lesson is the ladder's deliberate floor — the expert demonstrates two equally good behaviours, and the network converges to something no expert ever did. How and why it fails is what every later head is measured against.

The task

Two small problems, trained back to back in one run. T1 is one step: an obstacle sits at position \(c \in [-0.4,\, 0.4]\); the policy must output a 2-D waypoint that gets past it. T2 is closed loop: a point mass starts near (0, -1) and must reach a goal near (0, 1) without touching a disc of radius 0.3 near the origin; the action is a velocity in [-1, 1]² (0.04 per step) and the policy emits a chunk of horizon future actions at a time.

The data

The expert is scripted and bimodal. In T1 it passes the obstacle on the left or the right with equal probability: \(a = (c \pm 0.5,\ 0.5) + \text{noise}\). In T2 it picks a side at random per episode and holds it. Both sides are equally good, so both are in the data; the Expert noise knob sets the Gaussian jitter on every demonstrated action.

The method

Behaviour cloning is supervised regression: given the observation, predict the expert's action chunk. The loss, with the padding mask for chunks that run past the end of an episode:

$$L = \frac{\sum_h m_h \cdot \operatorname{mean}_d\!\left[(\hat a_{h,d} - a^*_{h,d})^2\right]}{\sum_h m_h}$$

where \(m_h\) is the padding mask, \(\hat a\) the predicted chunk and \(a^*\) the expert chunk.

The network is the same one the later tasks use, but it is fed zeros instead of a noisy chunk, and the time embedding is fixed at \(t = 0\), so it cannot do anything except map the observation to one answer. Minimising squared error then makes that answer the conditional mean of the expert's actions. With two equally likely modes at \(c - 0.5\) and \(c + 0.5\), the mean is \(c\): straight into the obstacle.

Motivation — why each technique is in this experiment

The MSE head — the deliberate floor. The baseline every later head is measured against. The point is not to make it work but to make it fail for a stated reason: squared error fits the conditional mean, and with a bimodal expert the mean is an action nobody demonstrated (100% of T1 samples inside the obstacle).

The same net as the generative heads. The Regression head feeds the shared MLPDenoiser zeros where the next lesson feeds a noisy chunk, and pins the time embedding at \(t = 0\). Whatever the next lesson gains over this one is then the head, never capacity.

Two sub-tasks, T1 and T2. T1 is one step, so the mode average is directly visible as the scatter on \(a_x = c\); T2 is closed loop, where feedback partially corrects the averaged chunk (87% success at H = 16 in the tutorial, against T1's 100% collision). The pair is the warning that open-loop and closed-loop numbers do not move together.

Action chunking. The Action chunk knob (1/8/16) is here because single-step policies compound their own errors. In this task the whole chunk is executed; partial execution (n_exec) only arrives with the generative head.

Z-scoring and EMA. Observations are normalised with the training set's statistics, and evaluation uses an EMA copy (0.999) of the weights — shared plumbing of the whole ladder, so the measured failure is the estimator's property, not scaling or optimiser jitter.

Theory — the loss picks the estimator

Behaviour cloning turns control into supervised learning, and the loss then decides which statistic of the expert the network becomes. Squared error is not a neutral default: for each observation, the action that minimises the expected squared error is the conditional mean,

$$\arg\min_{a}\ \mathbb{E}\!\left[\lVert a - a^{*}\rVert^{2} \mid s\right] \;=\; \mathbb{E}\!\left[a^{*} \mid s\right],$$

which is exactly the quantity Regression trains the denoiser to output. The mean is the right answer only when the expert's conditional distribution has one peak. In T1 it has two, so the optimum is \(\tfrac12(c - 0.5) + \tfrac12(c + 0.5) = c\) — an action no expert ever demonstrated. More data or a bigger network cannot help: the network is converging to precisely what the loss asks for. Swapping the loss moves the failure without removing it — L1 regression gives the conditional median, which for two symmetric modes still sits between them, and a Gaussian likelihood adds a variance around one peak. The structural fixes either score actions instead of regressing them (implicit BC's energy-based model), sample from the distribution (the flow and diffusion heads of the next lesson), or keep regression but route the mode through a latent variable (ACT's CVAE).

Two design choices here are deliberate. The baseline reuses the same MLPDenoiser as the generative heads — zeros for the noisy chunk, the \(t = 0\) embedding — so any difference you measure against the next lesson is attributable to the head alone, not to capacity. And actions are predicted in chunks because single-step policies compound their own errors: each small deviation drifts the policy into states slightly further from the data, where the next prediction is worse. A chunk cuts the number of decisions per episode; its price, less reactivity, returns as a knob in the final lesson.

The architecture

B = batch, H = action chunk (1 in T1, Action chunk in T2), 2 = action dims. The loss node sits where the network output meets the dataset's expert chunk; nothing else trains the net. Later tasks replace the zeros with a noisy chunk and the time embedding with the real one — the rest stays.

Regression head: no noise in, the chunk out, MSE loss
class Regression:
    # ...
    def loss(self, model, x0, cond, mask=None):
        # B = batch, H = chunk horizon, A = action dims; x0 [B, H, A] is the expert chunk
        # the net sees x_t = zeros [B, H, A] and t = zeros [B]; out [B, H, A] is the chunk itself
        out, extra = model(torch.zeros_like(x0), torch.zeros(x0.shape[0], device=x0.device), cond)
        err = (out - x0).pow(2).mean(-1)  # [B, H, A] -> [B, H]
        if mask is not None:
            err = err * mask  # [B, H], padding zeroed
            return err.sum(1) / mask.sum(1).clamp_min(1), extra  # [B, H] -> [B]
        return err.mean(1), extra  # [B, H] -> [B]

Closed loop: sample a chunk, play its first actions
def act(self, obs) -> np.ndarray:
    # N = parallel episodes, H = chunk horizon, A = action dims
    if self.queue is None or self.k >= self.n_exec:
        cond = self.ds.cond(self.make_cond(obs))  # dict of normalised [N, ...] tensors
        n = next(v for v in cond.values() if torch.is_tensor(v)).shape[0]  # N
        shape = (n, self.H, self.ds.t["x0"].shape[-1])  # [N, H, A]
        # ...
        self.queue = self.queue.clamp(-1, 1).cpu().numpy()  # chunks [N, H, A]
        self.k = 0
    a = self.queue[:, self.k]  # [N, H, A] -> [N, A], action k of every chunk
    self.k += 1
    return a

What to watch

Both losses fall smoothly and converge, and that is the trap: a low loss says nothing about whether the policy can do the job. Watch T1 collision. In this repo's tutorial T1 run (docs/ta_tutorial.md, 20k steps, 3 seeds) the MSE head put 100% of its samples inside the obstacle while a flow head put 3.0% there. The scatter below shows why: the predictions cluster on the line \(a_x = c\), between the two expert modes at \(c \pm 0.5\), inside the shaded band. In closed loop (T2) the effect is milder, since the mean of two half-chunks is partially corrected on the next step (tutorial T2, MSE with H = 16: 87% success vs DDPM 67.5%). Do not assume the two numbers move together.

Knobs

  • Demonstrations: how many expert episodes are collected. More demos shrink the noise in the mean but cannot split it into modes.
  • Training steps: optimiser steps per part (T1 and T2 each get this many). Loss keeps improving long after the behaviour stops changing — compare the curves with collision.
  • Learning rate: step size of AdamW with warmup and cosine decay. Too high and the loss spikes; too low and the run is undertrained at the step budget.
  • Batch size: samples per gradient step. Mostly changes loss noise and speed, not the mode-averaging.
  • Action chunk (T2): how many future actions the net predicts at once. Longer chunks commit to a side for longer but make the averaged chunk bend further into the obstacle.
  • Expert noise: jitter on demonstrated actions. Higher noise blurs the data and widens the prediction error without changing the fact that the mean lies between the modes.

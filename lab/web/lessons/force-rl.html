The question

Lessons 16–18 trained every policy by imitation, and imitation has one blind spot this course has measured over and over: it copies what the expert did, never why. Nothing in a behaviour-cloning loss rewards stopping when the force says stop, or searching when the peg lands on the rim. This lesson keeps a trained nanoVLA frozen and lets a small residual learn on top of it, online, in the simulator, from a sparse reward — the ForceRFT recipe at nano scale. Can a few thousand parameters that read only the wrench and the base's own next action buy what the 7 M-parameter imitator could not — and where does that headroom actually live? Round 1 answers with reward alone; round 2 simulates what force-aware systems do at deployment — an operator who corrects the robot while the residual learns from those corrections.

The task

T3 peg insertion is the gate task (plan §8 M4). The hole sits up to ±4 mm away from the visible green marker on each axis — a sub-pixel offset at 96×96 — so a camera-driven base aims at the marker and lands on the rim. The information that would fix it is in the wrench: a peg sitting on the rim reads a vertical load with no depth progress, and a peg sliding into the chamfer reads the lateral push toward the hole. That is exactly the input a residual reads. Success = the peg tip seated below the hole top inside the opening within 20 s; |F| > 25 N aborts the episode.

Why not T1 (a measured result, kept on this page). Lesson 17 told the "BC never learns to stop" story on T1, so T1 was the first target. Before any RL we evaluated two frozen T1 bases on 40 held-out seeds and split the failures:

55–58% of all episodes are lost to the wrong button. A residual that sees no image and no instruction cannot know which button is right (the sides swap at random), so its ceiling on T1 is the force-failure share: 1–3 episodes in 40, i.e. 2.5–7.5 points — below the +10 bar before a single gradient step. Two 5 k-step pilot runs confirmed it (success flat at 25%, while the residual already grew 4× larger in contact than in free space). The evaluation now reports success_cond — success among episodes not lost to a non-force task failure — which is the number a force residual can move. T1 stays a parameter; the gate moved to T3, the plan's original choice.

The method: a bounded residual on a frozen VLA

Residual composition. Every 8 env steps the frozen base plans a 16-step chunk from the cameras, the words and the state, exactly as in lesson 16 (its flow noise seeded per episode, so the base alone reproduces its own evaluation). At each 10 Hz step the residual reads the base's next action and adds a bounded correction:

$$dp_t = dp^{\text{base}}_t + \Delta_{\max}\,\tanh\!\big(\pi_\theta(o_t)\big),\qquad o_t = \big[s_t,\ F_t/10,\ (F_t - F_{t-1})/5,\ dp^{\text{base}}_t/0.01,\ g^{\text{base}}_t,\ \mathbb 1_{\text{task}}\big] \in\mathbb R^{28}.$$

No images: the base already looked; the residual reads what changes at contact and what the base intends. The gripper is never corrected. The base keeps re-planning from the real, residual-perturbed state — the residual corrects a closed loop, not a replay.

The composition: base action + bounded residual (bit-exact base at r = 0)

Bounds scaled to step × stiffness. v1 of the plan bounded the residual at ±5 mm per step. On the button the base presses at about 1 mm/step into a 1000 N/m spring: 1 mm is 1 N, so one exploratory ±5 mm step adds 5 N on top of a 3–9 N threshold and jumps straight through the 1.5× band. Δmax = 2 mm is enough to cancel the press and back off at 1 mm/step, and enough to cover T3's ±4 mm offset in two steps, while every step stays a bounded deviation (≤ 2 N into a spring). A residual on the force target (±2 N) exists behind residual_force for a hybrid-head base; today's bases are position-head, so the flag refuses them rather than learning a correction nothing applies.

Zero start. The actor's last layer is scaled by 0.01 and its bias zeroed (lesson 13's recipe), so at step 0 the residual is < 5% of its bound (< 0.1 mm) and the policy is the base; with the last layer fully zeroed the executed actions equal the base's bit for bit — a unit test.

TD3 + BC toward zero, with a floor. The learner is lesson 11's TD3 unchanged (fvb.policy.td3: twin critics, the minimum of the two as target, delayed actor updates, target-policy smoothing). The stored action is the residual. The actor loss adds lesson 12's BC term, but its target is residual = 0 on the states the policy visits: "behave like the base unless the critic says otherwise". Its weight anneals from 2.5 to a floor of 1.0 and never further — lesson 12 measured what happens at zero (the actor drifts off the prior as soon as the critic is wrong, which early on it is), and the code refuses floors below 1.0.

The BC schedule: anneal, never to zero
    if floor < BC_FLOOR_MIN:

Reward. Sparse, as v1 specified: +1 on success, −1 on a force abort (or T1's over-force), −0.01 per step, and an optional peak-force penalty paid as the episode's peak |F| rises (summed over an episode it is exactly k·peak/25 N — "gentler" stated as a goal). A timeout bootstraps: the features carry no clock, so running out of time is not a property of the state. An optional, privileged hole shaping term (lesson 13's potential toward the true hole, off by default) is the one deviation tried on this page, and it is analysed below.

The loop. Four envs per seed, stepped round-robin (each with its own episode, chunk and noise stream), UTD 0.5 after a 1 k-step warm-up, Gaussian exploration of 0.2× the bound around the actor's output, evaluation every 4 k env steps on 20 fixed held-out seeds with the residual deterministic. Five RL seeds run as five one-thread processes in parallel; the final 100-seed evaluation rolls the frozen base and every residual on the same seeds.

Lazy rendering — the throughput lever. A profiled CPU step was ~33 ms, of which the two 96×96 osmesa renders took 26 ms, physics 0.9 ms and a TD3 update 6–9 ms. Only the base reads images, and only every 8th step — so the env renders only on the steps the base will plan from. Throughput went from 33.7 to 66.5 env steps/s, and the physics is provably untouched (outcomes and peak forces identical with it on and off).

Motivation — why each choice is in this experiment

Why a residual and not fine-tuning. The base is a flow model whose actions come out of a 10-step ODE; pushing RL gradients through it is expensive and destabilising, and it can unlearn what imitation got right. A residual leaves the base intact (its evaluation is reproducible bit for bit), learns in a 28-D input space where a few thousand transitions are enough, and fails safe: its worst case is bounded by Δmax.

Why the BC term points at zero, and has a floor. In TD3+BC (lesson 12) the BC target is the demonstrator's action; for a residual the demonstrator is the base, whose residual is zero by definition. The floor is lesson 12's hardest-won number: annealing BC to zero let the actor follow an immature critic off a cliff.

Why the wrench and its change are inputs. They are what the base cannot use well: lesson 17 fed force into the VLA and it barely moved the needle at this scale; the residual reads it directly, at every step, with a reward that says what to do with it.

Why five seeds, reported one by one. Lessons 11–13 measured RL convergence as a seed lottery — a bimodal set of finals where the mean curve describes no seed. The board's last row lists every seed's final.

Why the T1 headroom table is on this page. A claim bar fixed before the run is only honest if the run can reach it. Splitting the base's failures first showed that it could not on T1, and the gate moved to the task whose missing information is in the wrench.

Theory — residual RL on a frozen VLA: ForceRFT, and DSRL as the black-box alternative

ForceRFT (arXiv 2609.22840) refines a frozen force-conditioned SmolVLA on a real robot (plug insertion, ring-on-peg, whiteboard wiping): a lightweight residual actor refines individual end-effector pose commands from the wrist wrench, its change and the base’s selected motion; human corrections supervise the actor and only verified autonomous transitions train the twin critics (its abstract states no step budget). This lesson is its nano version: same structure (frozen base, bounded residual on the next action, twin-critic off-policy learner, a prior that pulls the residual home), fewer dimensions (3-D position residual; the force-target residual is wired but needs lesson 18's hybrid head).

Why TD3 is the right learner here. Deterministic actor, off-policy replay (every transition reused ~0.5 times per step at UTD 0.5), clipped double-Q against the overestimation that sparse rewards invite, and delayed actor updates so the actor chases a critic that has had time to settle (Fujimoto et al. 2018).

DSRL (arXiv 2506.15799) is the cleanest alternative: learn a policy over the flow model's input noise ε with SAC and never touch the base's output at all — the base becomes a black box that maps noise to actions, and RL steers which of its own modes it samples. It cannot produce an action the base could not, which is both its safety argument and its ceiling (no offset correction the base never demonstrated). Not implemented in this lesson: the base already exposes ε through act(generator=...), so it is a natural extension, but every number on this page is the residual.

How to read it. With the sparse reward the training episodes collected ≤ 2% successes in 20 k steps (every seed), the critic converged to a flat value (Q ≈ −0.35, loss ~5·10-4) and the BC term at its floor did exactly what it is for: with no evidence that deviating pays, it held the residual at 2–3% of its bound. That is the safe failure mode of a residual — it never made the base worse — and it is also why m4.residual_quiet_in_free_space cannot be read as "acts at contact": contact and free-space magnitudes are both noise-level. The shaped variant shows the opposite failure. Shaping toward the true hole is lesson 13's recipe, but there the actor's observation carried a hole estimate; here the residual sees only the wrench, the state and the base's intent, so the shaping reward is unpredictable from its inputs, the critic's loss stays 300× higher (0.22–0.27), the residual grows largest in free space (0.55–0.65 mm/step) and the force aborts rise above the base's. A reward term that the policy cannot observe the cause of is noise with a sign. The lesson's actual finding: runtime RL refines a policy that already succeeds sometimes (ForceRFT starts from a base that works most of the time); it does not discover a skill whose key state is invisible to it from a 5% base in 20 k sparse-reward steps. The next experiments that would move the board are named in the exercises below, not tuned in.

Throughput, for budgeting: on an idle host one worker runs ~66 env steps/s (1 thread, lazy rendering); here 8 workers shared the 24 cores with two other jobs (load average ~70) and ran 17–20 env steps/s each — 24 minutes of wall time for 5 × 20 k steps, every seed in parallel.

Round 2 — simulated deployment: an operator corrects, the residual learns

Round 1 measured why a sparse reward alone cannot lift a 2 % base: training saw almost no successes, so there was nothing to propagate. Force-aware systems that do this on real robots do not rely on reward alone. Read across their abstracts, the residual at deployment serves four purposes — and the fourth is the one round 1 lacked:

  1. React to force inside a chunk. The VLA plans a chunk; the residual corrects every control step from the live wrench, its change and the base’s selected motion — ForceRFT (arXiv 2609.22840). Lesson 17 measured the gap this fills: one force sample per 0.8 s chunk arrives too late.
  2. Add force-awareness without retraining the base. CR-DAgger’s residual adds a force input and even target forces on top of a position-only base (arXiv 2506.16685).
  3. Learn within robot time. A small off-policy actor next to a strong prior: ResFiT reports 14–23 % → ~64 % with 15–76 minutes of robot data (arXiv 2509.19301); HIL-SERL trains real-robot policies in 1–2.5 h (arXiv 2410.21845).
  4. Learn from human corrections. In ForceRFT, human corrections supervise the residual actor; only verified autonomous transitions train the twin critics, and bootstrapping stops at intervention boundaries.

This round simulates that deployment on T3. A privileged operator (the scripted expert, which can see the true hole — the stand-in for a person who can) watches every episode and takes over when the peg sits in contact for 1 s without 1 mm of depth, or at once above 12 N; it may hold at most 20 % of all steps. Its nudges are stored as corrections in the residual’s own units. The robot acts with a snapshot of the actor, swapped every 500 updates, as a background learner on hardware would.

Amber blocks are what round 2 adds. The dashed arrow from the operator replaces the residual while it holds control; the dashed arrow from the swap is the only way learned weights reach the robot.

The operator's nudge, expressed in the residual's own units
def correction(operator_dp: np.ndarray, base_dp: np.ndarray, dp_res: float) -> np.ndarray:
    """What the operator's nudge is in the residual's own units.

    legend: A = 3 residual dims (dp xyz); dp_res = the residual bound (m per axis per step)
        operator_dp [3] m   what the operator wants executed this step
        base_dp     [3] m   what the frozen base is about to execute
        returns     [A]     float32 in [-1, 1]: executed dp = base_dp + dp_res * correction
    """
    c = (np.asarray(operator_dp, np.float64) - np.asarray(base_dp, np.float64)) / dp_res  # [3]
    return np.clip(c, -1.0, 1.0).astype(np.float32)  # [3] -> [A], bounded like any residual
In the loop: while the operator holds control, its correction replaces the actor's residual
base_dp = train_actor._base_next(sl, replan=False)[0]  # [3] m, base's next dp
r = correction(sl.op.operator_dp(sl.env), base_dp, train_actor.dp_res)  # [A]

Two learners, two rules. The actor imitates the operator on intervention rows and the base (residual 0) on autonomous rows, half and half, plus TD3’s Q term. The critics learn only from autonomous segments and never bootstrap across a take-over:

The actor's BC target: the correction on operator rows, zero on autonomous rows

The boundary cut: no TD target may bootstrap into an operator take-over
def mark_boundary(self, idx: int) -> None:
    """The autonomous transition ``idx`` led into an operator take-over: its o2 is the
    state the operator took over in, so no TD target may bootstrap from it -> not a
    critic sample (it still feeds the actor's BC-toward-0)."""
    if idx >= 0 and not self.intervened[idx]:
        self.critic_ok[idx] = False

def critic_idx(self) -> np.ndarray:
    """Indices the twin critics may sample: autonomous, not cut at a boundary."""
    return np.flatnonzero(self.critic_ok[: self.n])

What the residual learned. What did not improve — a measurement lesson

Autonomy did not rise (NOT CONFIRMED): the operator’s share of steps was 20.3 % in the first fifth of training and 20.0 % in the last. Tech development — what broke, how it was found, what changed

Every change that was needed to make this lesson's experiments and claims work, in the order it was found: symptom, how it was found, cause, change, take-away. Master copy: docs/force_vla_school_techdev.md.

19.1 — on the button task the residual had nothing to fix. Symptom: two 5k-step RL runs left T1 success flat (0.25 → 0.25, 0.25 → 0.20). Found by: evaluating the frozen bases alone before any RL, with outcome counts — fvlmoe 17/40 successes, 22 wrong button, 1 force abort; the force-blind base 14/40, 23 wrong button, 3 force failures. Cause: a 1 mm/step press almost never overshoots, so 55–70 % of failures are the wrong button, and a residual that sees no image and no instruction cannot tell which button is right. The residual's ceiling on T1 is the force-failure share — 2.5–7.5 points. Change: lesson 19 moves to the peg task (T3), the plan's original choice, where the hidden hole offset is exactly what a wrench-reading residual can search for; the eval also reports success conditional on no non-force failure. Take-away: measure the base's failure anatomy before you choose what RL should fix.

19.2 — rendering, not learning, was the bottleneck. 33.7 env steps/s; timing each component showed 26 ms of a ~33 ms step went to rendering both cameras, though only the base reads images, and only every 8th step. Change: render only on the steps the base plans from → 66.5 env steps/s, with outcomes and peak forces verified identical.

19.3 — the plan's ±5 mm residual bound was too large. One exploratory step at 5 mm against a ~1000 N/m button is 5 N — enough to cross the over-force band of a 3 N threshold in a single step, so the residual would take over the press instead of correcting it. Change: ±2 mm. Take- away: scale a residual's bound to the base's step size times the contact stiffness.

19.4 — the thread count changes the outputs. The same base eval gave mean peak force 5.385013 at 4 threads and 5.385015 at 1 — CPU reductions summed in a different order. Change: RL runs pin torch_threads=1 and record it; a seed is reproducible only with the thread count fixed too.

19.5 — the behaviour-cloning target moved to visited states. For a residual the BC target is always "residual = 0", so demonstrations would only contribute their states — and building the base's next-action input for them would mean re-running the frozen base on every demo frame. Change: BC toward zero on the replay buffer's own states, through the demo slot TD3 already has.

19.6 — the BC floor is enforced in code. Lesson 12's failure (annealing the imitation weight to zero) is now impossible to configure by accident: floors below 1.0 are refused.

19.7 — a scratch file named profile.py broke torch. cProfile imports profile, which picked up the script; torch's optimizer import then failed with a misleading circular-import error. Take-away: never name scratch files after standard-library modules.

19.8 — the T3 base starts almost at zero. The base reached 0/20, 0/20, 1/20 at its periodic checks and 2/100 on the shared seeds (67 timeouts, 31 force aborts): the hole sits up to ±4 mm off the visible marker with 0.5 mm clearance, so a behaviour-cloned policy aims at the marker, lands on the rim and pushes. Consequence: with a success-only reward, 20k training steps saw at most 2 % successes, the critic went flat (Q ≈ −0.35) and the BC floor held the residual at 2–3 % of its bound. All four M4 bars stayed red (median gain +0 over 5 seeds). Take-away: runtime RL refines a base that already succeeds sometimes — ForceRFT starts from a working policy too; from a 2 % base, sparse reward has nothing to propagate.

19.9 — a reward the policy cannot observe is noise with a sign. A hole-direction shaping term (computed from the simulator's hidden hole position, reward-only) made everything worse: force aborts 37–60/100, peak force +15 %, the residual largest in free space, critic loss ~300× higher. Lesson 13's shaping worked because the actor observed a hole estimate; here it sees only wrench, state and the base's intent, so the shaped reward is unpredictable from its inputs. Kept as a documented knob, default off.

19.10 — the effect that did appear: push less. Every plain-reward seed cut force aborts from 31 to 10–18/100 (converted to timeouts) while success stayed flat; in 4 of 5 seeds the mean vertical residual in contact points upward (+0.013 to +0.031 mm/step, 1–3 mm less depth over a contact phase). The abort penalty was the only signal training actually collected, and the residual learned exactly that. It is what the 7 % peak-force drop measures.

19.11 — shared hosts cost 3× throughput. 17–20 env steps/s per worker against 66 on an idle box (load 70–76 from other jobs) — 5 seeds took 24 min instead of 12. Budget 25–30 min for five seeds on a shared machine.

19.12 — liveness shown for the wrong reason. Jobs started in fresh containers recorded pid 1, which in the lab container is its own always-alive init: the UI showed them as running by luck, and a server restart would mark them failed. Real runs belong to the live lab once the task is registered.

19.13 — the oversized bound does its damage while exploring. With a 5 mm residual bound, 20 % of the first training episodes ended in a force failure (0 % at 2 mm) and the contact residual reached 1.25 vs 0.40 mm/step — yet the deterministic eval was identical by 4k steps. On hardware the exploratory episodes are the ones that break things: watch the training force-failure rate, not the eval curve.

19.14 — reward alone had nothing to propagate; corrections did. Round 2 simulated a deployment: a privileged operator takes over when the peg stalls on the rim or the force passes 12 N (≤ 20 % of steps), its corrections supervise the actor, and the critics learn only from autonomous segments without bootstrapping across a take-over — ForceRFT's recipe. Same base, same seeds: median success 2 % → 42 % (+40; every seed +34 to +50) against +0 for reward alone, peak force −24 %. Take-away: the residual's purpose at deployment is to absorb a dense correction signal; from a weak base a sparse reward alone is not enough.

19.15 — an intervention rate that hits its budget measures the budget. The "autonomy rises" claim failed at a ratio of 0.99: the step-weighted operator rate sat at exactly the 20 % budget in every bin, because the triggers wanted more than the budget allowed. The robot-side signals did move (episodes the operator touched 73 % → 60 %; success among untouched episodes 48 % → 71 %). Take-away: report operator demand (triggers fired), not operator supply, or set the budget above demand.

19.16 — a trigger that fires on slowness can never step back. With an unlimited operator, the trained residuals were taken over in 86–98 % of episodes at the same step as the base, yet alone they succeed 36–52 %: "1 s in contact without 1 mm of depth" is also what a careful learned search looks like. A simulated operator should step in on failure (force rising toward abort, no progress for a long time), as HIL-SERL's humans do.

19.17 — imitated contact corrections leak into free space. The residual was no longer quiet in free space (0.40 vs 0.44 mm/step in contact): corrections exist only in contact states, and BC toward zero at a 50 % batch share did not pin the free-space output. Free-space states need more weight toward zero (and torque inputs scaled so the actor can read the hole direction).

19.18 — what the residual actually fills: a channel gap again. The base stalls ~15 mm deep in a hole it has found, with dp_z ≈ 0, because its demonstrations pushed through the force target a position-head base cannot emit. The operator pushes in position mode, and that vertical gap is what the residual learned — the same "which action channel carries the motion" lesson as 15.7 and 18.8.

Knobs

  • Frozen base: a lesson-16 nano-vla checkpoint trained on the chosen task. A suite checkpoint resolves its single non-blank arm on that task automatically; an ambiguous suite is refused with its arm labels.
  • Task: t3 (default, the gate) or t1 (the measured low-headroom case).
  • RL seeds / First seed: independent seeds, one 1-thread CPU process each, in parallel; seed s draws its training episodes from 100 000 + 100 000·s onward, outside every demo and eval block.
  • Env steps per seed: 20 000 is ForceRFT's budget and ~10–12 minutes of wall time here.
  • BC weight floor: the anneal 2.5 → floor runs over the first half of training; never below 1.0.
  • Peak-force penalty: k in the reward's −k·Δpeak/25 N term (exercise 2).
  • Hole shaping (privileged, T3): k in lesson 13's potential shaping toward the true hole, k·(lateral distance decrease)/4 mm, reward only — the actor and critic never see the hole. 0 is the plan's sparse reward; the k = 1 run on this page shows what it does when the cause is unobservable.
  • Residual bound: Δmax in mm per step per axis; 2 is the course value, 5 is v1's (exercise 3).
  • Residual on f_target: ±2 N on the base's force target; needs a hybrid-head (lesson 18) base and refuses a position-head one.
  • Eval every / Eval episodes per point: the in-training curves (shared fixed seeds, residual deterministic, point 0 = the frozen base).
  • Shared held-out seeds / Eval seed / video (evaluate): the base and every residual roll on the same seeds; the video plays the base and the median seed's residual on the same seed, one after the other.

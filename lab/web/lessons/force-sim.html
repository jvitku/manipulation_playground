The question

Lessons 16–19 will train policies that feel. Before any of them can be trusted, two smaller questions need honest answers on a testbed: what does a simulated wrist force/torque sensor actually measure — and why can a position controller, however well tuned, not hold a commanded contact force on a surface whose height it does not know? This lesson builds the testbed that answers both with numbers: a gantry with a wrist F/T sensor, four contact tasks whose deciding quantity is hidden from the camera, a position and a hybrid force/position controller, and scripted experts that record the demonstration datasets every later lesson trains on. It also introduces the claims board: each quantitative promise the course makes is a registered claim, and every evaluation fills in the ones it can measure with a CONFIRMED / NOT CONFIRMED / INCONCLUSIVE verdict.

The task

The robot is a rigid Cartesian gantry: three prismatic joints aligned with the world axes, a yaw hinge (position-held at 0 here) and a parallel gripper. A Cartesian step \(\Delta p\) is exactly a joint-space step — no arm kinematics between the policy and the contact physics. Between the z-carriage and the tool sits the wrist: a site with a force + torque sensor pair that sees everything distal to it. Two cameras (static + wrist) render at 96×96. Four tasks share this scene; each hides the one quantity that decides success where the camera cannot see it (the static camera covers ~0.6 m with 96 px, so 1 px ≈ 6 mm):

The method

The sensing chain. MuJoCo's site sensors report the raw wrench the wrist applies to everything below it, so at rest the sensor reads the tool's own weight, rotated into the sensor frame: \(m R^{\top}(-g)\). Subtracting that gravity wrench (the mass is the tool subtree's, the centre of mass is read exactly from the model every step, so finger motion cannot bias it) leaves the external contact wrench. It is then low-passed by a one-pole 20 Hz filter running at the 200 Hz controller rate; the controller and the policy both consume the filtered stream, nothing downstream sees raws. Sign convention, used everywhere and unit-tested: contact pushing up on the tool reads negative Fz (pressing on a table: Fz < 0); a mass resting on the tool reads positive. So "press down with 5 N" is a command of \(f_{t,z} = -5\) N.

The clock ladder. Physics steps at 2 ms (500 Hz); the controller ticks every 5 ms (200 Hz) — 200 does not divide 500, so one tick advances physics by 2, 3, 2, 3, … substeps (2.5 on average), and 20 ticks make exactly one 100 ms policy step (10 Hz). The wrench is sampled once per tick. What is invisible at control rate: an impact transient shorter than ~10 ms lives between 10 Hz policy samples and is attenuated by the 20 Hz filter — which is why the demos store each step's whole 200 Hz window (20×6), not just the last sample.

Position mode. The 10 Hz action \(\Delta p\) (clamped to ±5 cm per axis) is linearly interpolated to 200 Hz position targets driving MuJoCo position actuators — per axis a PD,

$$\tau = K_p\,(x_{cmd} - x) - d\,\dot x, \qquad K_p = 800\ \mathrm{N/m},\ d = 60\ \mathrm{Ns/m}.$$

In steady contact the actuator is a spring: the force is the command's overshoot below the surface times the series stiffness of actuator and pad, \(F \approx K_{\mathrm{eff}}\,\delta\) with \(1/K_{\mathrm{eff}} = 1/K_p + 1/K_{\mathrm{pad}}\). A position policy commands a penetration depth, not a force — and the depth it would need depends on the height it cannot see.

Hybrid mode — ForceVLA2 leaves its controller unspecified, so this is the course's written-down law (every number a deliberate choice, documented in controllers.py): once per policy step each axis is selected for force control, \(S_i = \mathbf{1}[\,|f_{t,i}| > 0.5\ \mathrm{N}\,]\), with 0.1 N of hysteresis — a selected axis stays selected until \(|f_{t,i}| < 0.4\) N. Only the force part can select: torque targets are ignored and yaw + gripper always run position mode. A selected axis moves by admittance at 200 Hz,

$$\dot x_i = \mathrm{clip}\!\bigl(K_a\,(f_{t,i} - f_i),\ \pm 5\ \mathrm{cm/s}\bigr), \qquad x_{cmd,i} \mathrel{+}= \dot x_i \cdot 5\ \mathrm{ms}, \qquad K_a = 0.01\ \mathrm{m/(s\,N)};$$

unselected axes follow their \(\Delta p\) interpolation. The admittance is integral action on the force error: the command keeps descending until the measured force balances the commanded one, so steady-state force tracking is exact regardless of \(K_p\), surface height or tool weight. Safety: |F| > 25 N anywhere aborts the episode as a failure — digging in can never score. All commands are clamped to the joint ranges, so a force error can never wind the command out of the workspace.

Motivation — why each piece is in this experiment

A gantry, not an arm. Prismatic world-aligned axes make \(\Delta p\) exactly a joint step: the contact physics is isolated from kinematics, \(F = K_{\mathrm{eff}}\,\delta\) is readable off a trace, and the plain-MJCF scene (gantry_xml.py builds the XML string) is itself lesson material.

Hidden quantities. Each task hides exactly the number that decides success below one camera pixel or in pure Python. That is the course's lever: lessons 17–18 must show force inputs/outputs beating vision alone, which is only measurable when vision alone cannot contain the answer.

96×96 osmesa (CPU) rendering. Measured 2.75 ms/frame (363 fps) in this image — a 200-demo task is about 3 minutes of rendering, and rendering never competes with training for the shared 10 GB GPU. The budget and the determinism both come from keeping the renderer off the GPU.

DART action noise (0.5 mm). While recording, the executed action is the expert action plus \(\mathcal N(0, 0.5\ \mathrm{mm})\) per axis, but the stored label stays clean (task 5 (force-token)'s convention). The observations visit states off the expert's exact path without corrupting the regression target — and the T3 expert's spiral-search recovery only appears in the data because the noise sometimes makes it miss.

The 25 N abort. Plan risk row: a position head could "solve" T2 by digging in. The abort makes over-forcing a visible failure mode instead of a silent win, on every task.

Demos store the 200 Hz window. Each policy step stores its full 20×6 wrench window: the click and jam transients the later force encoders need live between 10 Hz samples.

Claims as data. Expectations written as Claim rows (src/nfv/claims.py) get verdicts computed from measurements with confidence intervals, not prose — so "is force actually used" and its siblings are validated by default in every later lesson, and a failed expectation shows up red instead of unsaid.

Theory — quasi-static contact, admittance vs impedance, what an F/T sensor reads

Quasi-static contact under position control. A MuJoCo position actuator exerts \(\tau = K_p (x_{cmd} - x)\) (plus damping). Pressing a commanded depth \(\delta\) past a surface whose pad has stiffness \(K_{\mathrm{pad}}\), the tool rests where the spring forces balance; the two springs are in series, so

$$F = \frac{\delta}{1/K_p + 1/K_{\mathrm{pad}}} = K_{\mathrm{eff}}\,\delta.$$

Here the wipe tip's compliant contact (solref 0.02 ≈ 2 mm of give at 5 N, i.e. \(K_{\mathrm{pad}} \approx 2.5\) kN/m) in series with \(K_p = 800\) N/m gives \(K_{\mathrm{eff}} \approx 0.6\) N/mm: the ±8 mm hidden height alone moves the contact force by ≈ ±5 N — wider than T2's whole ±1.5 N tracking band. Measured on the testbed (fixed 5 mm press, Kp 800): 0.7 N on the nominal surface, 6.8 N when the surface is 8 mm high, no contact at all when it is 8 mm low.

Admittance and impedance in one paragraph. Both couple motion and force at an interface. An impedance controller takes measured motion and commands force (it renders a virtual spring/damper); an admittance controller takes measured force and commands motion — the natural choice on top of a stiff position-controlled machine, and what industrial arms usually do. Ours is the simplest useful admittance: pure integral action on the force error, rate-limited at 5 cm/s. Integral action is why the steady-state error is zero; the clamp plus the compliant pad is why it stays stable (a tenfold \(K_a\) still holds 5.00 N here, peak 5.09 N — measured).

What a wrist F/T sensor measures. Everything distal to it: contact wrenches, the tool's weight \(m R^{\top}(-g)\), and inertial terms when accelerating. "Trustworthy force readings" therefore means subtracting a load model — here the exact mass and per-step COM from the simulator (≤ 0.5 m/s and ~0.2 kg distal mass keep inertial spikes under the 1 N tolerances; a real sensor needs the least-squares identification this repo's Stage 0 built). A grasped object is not part of the subtree: its weight arrives as a contact wrench, exactly as on a real sensor.

The architecture

One 10 Hz policy step = 20 controller ticks = 50 physics steps. The orange feedback edge is the whole point of the hybrid mode: the filtered wrench re-enters the controller at 200 Hz, twenty times faster than any policy could react. Click a block's ⓘ for its purpose, shapes and update rate.

    def _load_now(self) -> tuple[LoadParams, np.ndarray]:
        """Current load (exact COM from the model) and the site->world rotation R."""
        r = np.asarray(self.data.site_xmat[self._site_id], dtype=np.float64).reshape(3, 3)
        com_world = np.asarray(self.data.subtree_com[self._body_id], dtype=np.float64)
        site_world = np.asarray(self.data.site_xpos[self._site_id], dtype=np.float64)
        com_site = r.T @ (com_world - site_world)  # (3,) COM offset in the sensor frame
        return LoadParams(mass=self.load_mass, com=com_site), r

    def compensated(self) -> np.ndarray:
        """Contact wrench (6,) = raw − tool gravity wrench, sensor-site frame, unfiltered."""
        load, r = self._load_now()
        return compensate(self.raw(), r, load)

    def sample(self) -> np.ndarray:
        """One 200 Hz pipeline sample: compensated then low-passed. Call once per ctrl tick."""
        return self._filter(self.compensated())

What to watch

All numbers below were measured in this image (the M0 gate run and this task's own eval scenarios, eval seed 0). Train records the demo datasets: at the defaults (4 tasks × 200 demos, 0.5 mm noise) about 7 minutes — measured 0.30/0.59/0.80/0.47 s per stored demo for T0/T1/T2/T3, ~0.2 MB each compressed — into lab/data/nfv/demos/<task>/, the canonical location lessons 16–19 read. The charts show per-task progress and the expert's success-of-tries (failed episodes are re-drawn, not stored).

Evaluate is the showcase, and the claims board at the top of the viewer is how to read it. Each scenario fills the claims it can measure and marks the rest "not measured in this run":

  • expert_rollouts → claim c1, the M0 gate: the scripted experts scored 100/100 on every task over 100 held-out seeds (Wilson 95% CI 96.3–100%), mean peak forces 3.3 / 6.0 / 7.7 / 11.1 N on T0/T1/T2/T3. At the default 20 episodes the same 20/20 comes back INCONCLUSIVE — the CI (83.9–100%) straddles the 95% gate, which is the board teaching you interval honesty; 100 episodes (~11 s) decide it.
  • hybrid_hold_5N → claim c2: commanded 5 N on plates hidden at −8/0/+8 mm, the hybrid holds mean Fn = 5.00 N at every height, max |mean error| < 0.01 N — integral action does not care where the surface is. Watch the trace: the admittance descends, touches, loads to −5 N and sits there; the mode strip is green (hybrid) throughout.
  • position_vs_hybrid_t2 → claim c3, the course's motivating claim, run as a two-arm comparison on the same seeds: the hybrid expert held T2's force band (≥80% of contact inside ±1.5 N) in 100/100 episodes; the position-only baseline — given the oracle press depth for the nominal height — in 15/100, exactly the episodes whose hidden height landed in its ~5 mm lucky window (band width / contact stiffness). Gap 85%, CI 73–91%: CONFIRMED already at the default 20 episodes (gap 90%, CI 54–97%). Mean in-band time 97.8% vs 32.8%.
  • kp_wipe_150_vs_800 is c3's mechanism made visible (it fills no claim — it has no hybrid arm): the same fixed-depth wipe at two stiffnesses. At Kp 150 the median contact force is 0.65 N — it barely touches anything (in-band 0.1% of contact, coverage 32%); at Kp 800 it presses whatever the height error dictates — 0.7 N on a nominal-height plate, 6.8 N on a +8 mm one, nothing on a −8 mm one (probe measurements). Soft gains press too little everywhere; stiff gains amplify the height error. No gain holds 3 N.

In the trace panel, the blue line is measured Fz, the dashed red line the commanded force (both negative when pressing — the sign convention), the green/grey strip the z-axis controller mode per step, and the markers show click / abort / seated events; scrub to read values and the expert's subtask label. T1 episodes are worth a scrub: the press is a pure position-mode descent at 1 mm per step — the mode strip stays grey and Fz climbs a ≈0.38 N-per-step staircase (\(K_{\mathrm{eff}} \times 1\) mm) until the episode ends at the click. The slowness is deliberate, twice over: the demos must carry the press in their dp labels (lesson 16's claims board caught an earlier hybrid-press expert whose press steps stored dp = 0, leaving a position-head policy nothing to clone), and the 10 Hz stop granularity must keep the post-click overshoot inside the success bound — measured with the threshold forced to its worst case 3 N over 100 seeds: peak/threshold at most 1.13, mean 1.00 (tests/nfv/measure_t1_overshoot.py), well under the 1.5× bound.

Exercises. (1) Run expert_rollouts at 100 episodes and watch c1 flip from INCONCLUSIVE to CONFIRMED — then try episodes = 5 and see how wide a 5/5 interval really is. (2) Run kp_wipe_150_vs_800 and read the two arms' tables and traces against \(F = K_{\mathrm{eff}}\,\delta\): the position traces sit flat at whatever force the hidden height dictates, and reversing direction (the coverage retry) changes nothing. (3) A deliberately wrong one: edit FT_5N in lab/tasks/task_nfv0.py to +5.0 ("force is positive, surely") and run hybrid_hold_5N. Measured: the tool climbs away from the plate at the 5 cm/s admittance clamp — z from 0.149 m to 0.296 m within 3 s, Fz pinned at −0.02 N, and the hold measures 0 N forever — because +5 N asks for a force pushing down on the tool, which only a weight resting on it could supply. The sign convention is not a formality; it is the difference between pressing and fleeing.

Knobs

  • Tasks to record (all / t0–t3) and Demos per task (default 200, the plan's dataset size): what Train writes to the canonical demo folders. Re-running overwrites a task's folder.
  • DART action noise (default 0.5 mm): executed-action noise while recording; labels stay clean. 0 gives pristine but narrow data; 2 mm visibly costs expert tries.
  • Seed: shifts the window inside each task's disjoint demo-seed block (eval seeds are a different block entirely — a policy never trains on an eval placement).
  • Scenario + Task (expert_rollouts only) + Episodes: per task / hidden height / arm. Claims want 100 episodes for decisive intervals; the physics-only scenarios run them in seconds.
  • Eval seed: shifts the eval-seed window, Render a rollout video: up to 4 episodes, spread over the scenario's groups, with the force strip under the camera view.

The checkpoint this task saves is a tiny "demo set" marker (the eval scenarios drive the scripted experts and controllers live and consume no model) — it exists because every lab evaluation names a checkpoint, and its payload records what was recorded, where, with which parameters. Task 16 (nano-vla) trains the first real model on these demos; the claims board introduced here is the instrument the whole force-VLA school is graded with.

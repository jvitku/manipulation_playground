Why not just fine-tune the flow policy with RL?

You have a decent policy from imitation (task 4, flow-bc). The natural next step is to improve it with reward. But a flow-matching policy does not hand you a likelihood or a one-step action: an action is the end point of an ODE integrated over \(K\) network calls, and it is only trained to match demonstrations. That makes the textbook policy-gradient and actor-critic recipes awkward. Two lines of recent work attack exactly this; both are literature context only, nothing in this lab reproduces them and none of their numbers are ours.

DPPO (Ren et al., “Diffusion Policy Policy Optimization”, arXiv 2409.00588) treats the denoising chain as an inner Markov decision process and runs PPO through it: every denoising step becomes a Gaussian action whose log-probability is available, so the policy gradient flows back through the whole chain. It works, but the effective horizon is the environment horizon times the number of denoising steps, which makes credit assignment and compute heavy.

FQL (Park et al., “Flow Q-Learning”, arXiv 2502.02538) avoids back-propagating through the ODE: it distils the multi-step flow into a one-step policy and trains that policy against the critic while a distillation term keeps it close to the flow (the behaviour prior). The price is a second network and a regularisation weight to tune.

Why a residual is the pragmatic first rung

The simplest way to leave the generative model alone is to not touch it. Freeze the flow policy and learn a small, cheap, ordinary actor that adds a correction:

$$a_t=\operatorname{clip}\big(a^{\text{base}}_t+\alpha\,\delta_t\big),\qquad \delta_t=\pi_\theta\big(o_t,\;a^{\text{base}}_t\big)\in[-1,1]^2$$

The critics see the same input \([o,a^{\text{base}}]\) and the stored action is \(\delta\) (not the sum), so the learning problem is an ordinary continuous-control TD3 problem whose “environment” happens to contain a good controller. The last layer of the actor starts near zero, so training begins at the base policy’s own behaviour and can only walk away from it if the critic says so.

What gets trained

The standard TD3 pair (Fujimoto et al., 2018; the repo’s fvb.policy.td3, the same one task 11 (td3) uses) with no demonstrations and no imitation term:

$$L_Q=\sum_{i=1,2}\big(Q_i(s,\delta)-y\big)^2,\quad y=r+\gamma\,(1-d)\min_i \bar Q_i\big(s',\,\bar\pi(s')+\varepsilon\big),\qquad L_\pi=-\,Q_1\big(s,\pi_\theta(s)\big)$$

The flow weights never appear in either loss. Residual scale \(\alpha\) is the safety dial: it bounds how far the correction can push (a delta of 1 moves the action by \(\alpha\)). A small \(\alpha\) is safe but can only polish the base; a large one can repair a bad base but also break a good one.

The reward (this lab’s own definition)

The repo has no residual-RL experiment to copy: scripts/42_ta_tutorial_t5.py (“T5”) is a supervised flow policy on MuJoCo insertion, not RL, and its recorded result is that the force token gave no measurable benefit (zeroed 34.7 % / suffix 35.3 % / suffix + aux 40.0 %, aux vs zeroed p = 0.40), with failures mostly timeouts. So the reward here is defined in the same spirit as the arm task’s (fvb.policy.rl_env, task 11, td3), on the contact world’s own signals:

  • depth progress: the change of the peg’s depth below the local surface level, scaled so that a full insertion is worth about 1;
  • potential-based shaping toward the true hole (privileged: only the reward sees it, the actor does not), weight Hole-shaping;
  • time cost every step; force penalty on the excess above half the abort level; +10 on success, −5 on a force abort.

Timeouts bootstrap (the observation does not know the clock); success and abort end the episode.

What to watch

  • The Success chart has a flat line, the frozen base’s success, measured once on the same fixed scenes at the start. The only question that matters is whether the residual curve climbs above it. A residual that sits on or under the line has learned nothing useful (or has hurt a base that was already good).
  • Residual size starts near 0. If it grows while success does not, the critic is chasing noise; check the Q estimate chart for divergence.
  • With a base that is already near-perfect there is no headroom; with a weak base (few steps, few demos) there is plenty. Try both, and compare Residual off in the evaluation.

How to read the evaluation

Evaluate once with the residual on and once with Residual off (the same checkpoint, the base alone) on the same seed: the same 400 scenes, so the difference is the residual. In the viewer the action grids show the base policy’s action, the residual that was added, and the summed action that reached the peg. The numbers from your runs are the result of this task; nothing on this page is a measurement. The checkpoint embeds the base weights it needs, so it still evaluates after the Flow-BC run it started from is deleted.
